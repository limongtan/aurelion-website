FICTIONAL WORKPLACE SCENARIO

Same meeting. Five different instincts.

A cross-functional team needs to decide whether a new process is ready to roll out. Choose a perspective to see what it adds to the decision.

O

Could we run a smaller pilot first?

A fresh route can preserve momentum while the team learns what works.

Try this in your next meeting

Ask for one alternative before the meeting moves to a decision.

N

Whose concerns have we missed?

People may agree in the room and still have reservations about what happens next.

Try this in your next meeting

Give each function a moment to name a concern or say they have none.

A

What would tell us the pilot is safe to expand?

An explicit threshold helps people distinguish evidence from assumptions.

Try this in your next meeting

Agree on the minimum evidence and circulate it in time for people to read.

R

Who needs to be brought into this?

The people who will use the new process can help make it workable.

Try this in your next meeting

Invite one person from an affected team to review the pilot plan.

D

Who will make the call, and by when?

A named decision owner and a deadline give the discussion a clear finish.

Try this in your next meeting

Name an owner now and put the review date in the calendar.

See how the team moves forward

A working agreement for this decision

  1. Send the evidence and remaining risks before the meeting.
  2. Hear a concern from each affected function and agree the smallest safe pilot.
  3. Name a decision owner and set a date to review the result.

This is an illustrative exercise. An LPI assessment looks at a blend of preferences across all five dimensions.

THE ORIGINS OF LPI

Developed by Professor Dr. Leonard Yong.

A former Universiti Malaya professor, Yong developed the Leonard Personality Inventory in the late 1990s to describe people’s preferred ways of behaving. Its five dimensions explore different contributions, from generating ideas to building agreement and making a decision.

  1. 1999

    Yong introduces the LPI model.

  2. 2003

    The LPI technical manual documents its development and research.

Sample growth report

Leading without taking over.

An illustrative report for a fictional workplace scenario.

In your own words

“When the deadline gets close, I rewrite the draft. Then I get frustrated when everyone waits for my approval.”

Your growth focus

Keep standards high while giving your team room to make decisions.

Your snapshot+

The rescue has become a routine.

You can see what could go wrong and move quickly to fix it. When that keeps pulling decisions back to you, the team has fewer chances to build its own judgement. Your opportunity is to make your judgement easier for others to use.

The pattern and blind spot+
  1. The deadline tightens
  2. You take the work back
  3. The team waits next time

“I rewrite the draft” and “everyone waits for my approval” may be connected. When you step in unexpectedly, people may start waiting for you on decisions you meant them to own. The next time you feel the urge to take over, ask who currently owns the decision.

A strength you can use+

You see trouble early.

You notice details that could put the work at risk. Before the next deadline, tell the owner what you’d watch for. Give them one signal that means they should call you and one decision they can make on their own.

A seven-day practice+
  1. Choose one decision

    Pick a decision the team can safely revisit. Agree who owns it, when to ask for help and when you’ll review it together.

  2. Ask before stepping in

    At the next check-in, ask “What do you recommend, and what risk are you watching?” Give the owner room to act within the agreement.

  3. Review at the end of the week

    Ask what moved forward without your approval and what guidance would have helped sooner. Use the answer to adjust how you hand off the next decision.

If you slip back into taking over

Tell the owner what you saw that made you step in. Ask them to make the next decision they can safely own.

A reminder to keep handy

“Whose decision is this? What support do they need from me?”
