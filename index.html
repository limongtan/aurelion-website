02The instrumentEveryone

Five dials. Every person in your building is a different setting of the same five.

The LPI measures five preferences. Not ability, not intelligence, not effort — preference. How a person would rather work if nobody stopped them. Scores run 0–100 and mean how strongly preferred, never how good. A child at 96 on one dial is not better than a child at 60; they will simply reach for a different route to the same answer.

Say it simply
Think of a mixing desk with five sliders. Everyone has all five — the question is only where each slider sits. Push one slider up and you gain something and pay for it somewhere else. The child who checks everything is slow. The child who finishes fast misses details. Neither is a flaw; both are a setting.
Why this matters commercially. Schools already spend on academic diagnostics — they know who is behind in Maths. Almost none can answer why this table of four produces nothing, or why that quiet child never speaks, or which of my six form teachers should take next year's difficult cohort. Those are the questions that cost you parents, staff and reputation. They are all temperament questions, and none of them appear in an exam result.

The Holistic Model — what you see is the Body; what drives it is the Mind

The LPI was never a standalone quiz. It is the measurement layer of Prof. Yong's Holistic Model of the individual — Spirit (pneuma) → Mind (psycho) → Body (soma). Read it right to left, the way you meet a child: the ONARD behaviour you can see lives in the Body — it is the surface. Driving each visible dial is a core emotional need in the Mind:

Oneeds Creativity Nneeds Stability & security Aneeds Accuracy Rneeds Recognition Dneeds Achievement

When the need is fed, the Body shows the strengths column of a child's report — new ideas, calm, precision, optimism, drive. When it is starved, the same dial shows its stress face — restless, fearful, fussy, careless, aggressive. Underneath both sits the Spirit layer: wellness. Emotional Excellence (EE) spans all three layers; ordinary EQ only reaches Mind and Body — which is why the LPI is one of the few instruments designed inside a whole-person development model rather than as a labelling exercise. That single idea — behaviour as a need being met or missed, never a character verdict — is the lens this whole document is written through.

06The measure everything else usesTeacher · Principal

"Wavelength gap" — the one number behind every recommendation in this document. Here is exactly what it means.

Every child sits somewhere on the map you saw at the top. The wavelength gap between two people is simply how far apart they sit — how differently they are wired. It runs from 0 to about 75 in a class this size. A small gap is not automatically good and a big gap is not automatically bad, which is the part everybody gets wrong, so the four bands below spell out what each one actually predicts.

Pick any two students — watch the gap get measured
Say it simply
Small gap = comfortable. They understand each other without trying — and they will agree too fast and miss things. Big gap = powerful but expensive. They see what the other cannot — and it will cost you a written rule and some supervision. The skill is not avoiding big gaps. It is knowing which big gaps to spend your attention on.

Where you'll see this number again

The pair matrix uses it to sort all 15 pairings. The grouping lab uses the widest gap in a table to predict whether that table needs supervision. The teacher's reach index uses her gap to each child. The principal's fit matrix uses the average gap between a teacher and a whole class.

It is one idea, applied four times. Learn it once here and the rest of the document reads itself.

The second number: coverage

Gap tells you how hard it will be. Coverage tells you whether the group can do the job at all — how many of the five dials are held by somebody at 80 or above. A pair can be perfectly comfortable and still be unable to finish anything.

Gap × coverage is the whole grouping engine. Comfortable and complete runs itself. Far apart and complete is powerful but needs a script. Far apart and incomplete needs you standing there.

10For the teacher & her headTeacher · Principal

Pn. Fong Mei is the warmest adult in the building. That is not the same as being the right fit for this room.

This is the chapter that only works if it is honest, so here it is plainly. FM's own report reads Openness 76 · Harmony 82 · Precision 78 · People-energy 80 · Drive-to-finish 76 — the Helpful Encourager. Approachable, warm, loyal, deeply invested in everyone getting along. Her report also names, in her own answers, that she lets small troubles grow into a crisis before acting firmly, compromises to avoid conflict, is not a goal-setter, and is disorganised with time.

Now hold that beside a class whose scarcest quality is finishing and whose widest split is people-energy. Her temperament matches this room's weakness instead of covering for it. That is a staffing insight, not a character judgement — and it is fixable with furniture and routine rather than personality change. Every one of the four moves below converts something her report flags as a weakness into a piece of classroom architecture that does the work for her.

Her own report — strengths, stress, conflict, creativity, how she learns — plus how she reads this specific class.

Her reach — who she gets to without trying, and who she has to reach for

Her temperament reach to each child — how far her own instincts have to travel to meet them, in reach points (0 = identical wiring). A short bar is not favouritism and a long bar is not dislike. Short means her instincts already work on that child: she will naturally call on them, read their mood correctly, and enjoy them. Long means instinct will quietly under-serve them — not from unkindness, but because the signals that child sends are not the signals she is tuned to receive. Long bars need a written rule in the lesson plan. That is the entire fix.

The finding that matters

JG is four and a half times further from her than AT is.

AT sits 12 points of wavelength away from FM. JG sits 54. Same class, same lesson, same adult — and one of them is operating in a room built around a temperament he does not share, in front of an adult whose instincts point elsewhere.

FM learns best in a group, prefers a slow, non-pressurising pace, and is told by her own report that she is not good with a lot of detail. JG needs logical structure, a source cited before he accepts a fact, and depth over coverage. SM prefers to work alone. Left to instinct she will teach the way ED, AT and ZW learn — and quietly under-serve the two children who need structure most.

This is not about liking them less. It is a warm, group-paced, talk-it-through adult finding a private, precise, evidence-first child harder to read — and reading his quiet as contentment.

What FM herself needs to grow — and what it unlocks for each child

A teaching plan that only fixes the children is half a plan. These four are drawn from FM's own report, in her own words, and each one is scored against which of the six children it directly helps. This is also the exact content of the coaching cycle we sell — six sessions, one per fortnight, built from her own profile rather than a generic course.

How she should teach each one

Six differentiated approaches, built from each child's report and hers together. The opening move, the format that gets through, the feedback language that lands, the thing to never do, and this term's one countable target.

The rules that apply to every child in the class

Before the individual cards — six things that are true for all six children in this room, and one hard rule that matters more than the rest of the document put together.

✓Do this

  • Describe, don't praise. "You checked that twice before handing it in" beats "you're so clever". Five of these six children are flagged as needing approval — general praise feeds the need; specific observation builds the skill.
  • Correct in private, always. Five of six are named in their own reports as easily hurt by criticism. The same sentence delivered at the dinner table in front of a sibling lands as humiliation and gets stored, not processed.
  • Ask about the method, not the mark. "How did you work that out?" is the single highest-value question a parent of any of these six can ask. It rewards thinking rather than outcome, and it works on all five settings.
  • Protect one finish a week. All six are told to finish what they start. One boring task, completed to standard, weekly — not more homework, just one thing seen through.
  • Name the feeling before the fix. "That looked frustrating" first. Then the solution. Skipping straight to the fix reads as dismissal to a harmony-seeking child.
  • Tell the teacher what you see at home. The stress signals in these reports show up at home first, almost always. A parent who reports "she's gone quiet and agreeable" gives the teacher two weeks of warning.

✕Never do this

  • Never tell a child their score or their type. Not the number, not the label, not "you're the analytical one". A thirteen-year-old hears a number as a ceiling and a label as a life sentence. This is non-negotiable and it is in our contract.
  • Don't compare siblings by setting. "Your brother just gets on with it" tells a low-drive child that the way they are built is a fault. It is not a fault; it is a dial, and dials move with practice.
  • Don't use the profile to excuse. "He can't help it, he's easily bored" removes the growth target. The report names the weakness so it can be worked on, not so it can be filed as permanent.
  • Don't demand eye contact or enthusiasm as proof of respect. For a low people-energy child, performing warmth costs real energy that then isn't available for the work.
  • Don't fill the silence. A quiet child processing is not a child refusing. Count to seven before rephrasing the question.
  • Don't ask "did you have a good day?" It gets "fine" from all six. Ask "what was the hardest bit?" instead — it gets an answer from every one of them.
The one conversation to have with every parent

"Your child is not being difficult. A setting is being starved."

A child who loves new things and is bored is not disrespectful — the dial that reaches for the unfamiliar has nothing to reach for. A low people-energy child who resists group work is not antisocial — that dial is genuinely set low, and being made to perform warmth costs them real energy. A high-harmony child who has gone silent after a correction is not sulking — the dial that keeps the peace has just registered a rupture and is protecting it.

Reframing behaviour as a starved setting is the entire parenting shift, and it takes one sentence to teach.

And what to say instead of a score

Talk about the behaviour and what helps it — never the label. This is how we train every parent in the workshop, and it is the difference between a tool and a stigma.

Say: "You do your best thinking when you've had time to check it first. Let's build that in."
Never: "You scored low on people skills."

13For the students themselvesStudent

Six cards written for thirteen-year-olds. No scores. No labels. No ceilings.

Here is the constraint we set ourselves, and it is the most important design decision in this document: a student card may never contain a number, a type name, or a comparison to a classmate. A thirteen-year-old handed a low score on a dial will carry that number as a ceiling for years. So these cards talk only about things you do and things you could try — because behaviour is changeable and a label is not.

What each card gives a child: one thing they are genuinely good at and would never have claimed for themselves, one thing to stretch stated as an action rather than a fault, one specific classmate they can help and how, and one way to make their teacher's job easier. That last one is deliberate. Children who understand they can help an adult behave differently in a room.

This is read out on day one of the student session. It takes ninety seconds and it does more for a classroom than any poster on the wall — because it gives every child in the room a job, and a job is the opposite of a label.

Why we insist on running the student session

A school can buy the reports and stop there. Most do. The measurable change happens when the children know. Not their scores — the language. A class that can say "I think this table needs a finisher" or "give me a minute, I check things twice" has absorbed the whole framework without ever seeing a number.

It also removes the teacher as the sole translator. Six children who can name what they need are six fewer misreads per lesson.

What we will not do with students

No scores, no archetype names, no "find your type" activity, no comparison exercises, no ranking, no wall display. We have turned down this request before and we will turn it down again — including from paying schools.

If a school wants a personality poster for the corridor, we are the wrong supplier. The restraint is the product. It is also what makes it safe to run on minors at scale.
