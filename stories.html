It Forgot It Could Fly

It forgot it could fly.

A farmer found an eagle’s egg at the foot of a mountain. He put it in the chicken coop, to hatch with the chicks.

The little eagle grew up pecking and walking with the chickens. It thought it was a chicken too.

Once, it spread its wings. The chicks laughed. “Don’t be silly. We can’t fly.”

So it folded its wings, and never opened them again.

Year after year, it grew bigger than every chicken. But every day, it kept its head down, looking for grain.

Until one day, an eagle flew across the sky. It looked up for a long time. And for some reason, it hurt.

A traveller passing by knew what it was, and carried it up the mountain.

“The wind has stopped,” he said. “Want to try?”

He let go. And it fell, and fell…

At the very last moment, its wings opened on their own. It flew.

Many people don’t leave. Not because they aren’t good enough. But because a familiar place feels safe, and change feels too hard.

Stay somewhere long enough, and everyone tells you: it’s dangerous out there. It’s safer here.

Stay long enough, and you might forget. You can fly, too.

So where’s your coop? And when did you last open your wings?

What have you stopped trying?

The Milk or the Hug

One mom had all the milk. The other just had a hug.

Two moms lived in the little monkey’s room. One was made of wire, with a bottle of milk on her chest that never ran dry.

The other was soft cloth. She had nothing to give. She was just soft, and warm.

His dad left before sunrise and got home after midnight. He figured a fed kid was enough.

So the bottles got bigger, and the wire mom got fancier.

But the little monkey only went to the wire mom when he was hungry.

The rest of the time, he held on to the cloth mom. He talked to her, slept on her, held on to her even in his dreams.

Dad came home, saw his kid hugging a piece of cloth, and it bugged him.

“Must be the milk,” he thought. So he bought a pricier bottle.

That night, thunder struck. The little monkey ran right past the wire mom. He didn’t even slow down.

He dove into the cloth mom’s arms and wouldn’t let go.

Dad rushed home through the rain and found him there, shaking. The bananas slipped from his hands.

When his child was scared, he didn’t want milk. He wanted a hug.

Is that only a story? In 1958, Harry Harlow tested it with real baby monkeys. Even when the wire mother had the milk, they spent most of their time on the cloth one. When scared, they usually ran to her.

Dad wasn’t a bad dad. He worked that hard because he loved him. That’s exactly why he missed it.

Being responsible is your strength. Overdo it, and you only provide. You forget to be there.

That night, Dad knelt on the floor and held him. Nothing to give. Just there.

So what do you remember from when you were small: the milk, or the hug? And who is waiting for yours?

Are you giving, or being there?

The Fifth Egg

He’s three times her size. And she can’t stop feeding him.

Three weeks ago, Mae had four little eggs in this nest. One morning, while she was out, a stranger slipped in a fifth.

It hatched first. And the first thing that chick did was roll her eggs over the edge, one by one.

Mae came home and never noticed. She saw one baby, and she loved him.

So she fed him, from sunup to sundown. Catch a bug. Fly back. Catch a bug. Fly back.

He got bigger. She got thinner. Her feathers started falling out.

She used to sing every morning, right at the top of the reeds. Not anymore. There was no time.

“Mae, look at him,” her mate said. “That’s not our baby.”

“He’s hungry,” she said. “Can’t you hear him?”

One night she came back shaking, with nothing in her beak. He screamed. She flew back out into the dark.

So why couldn’t she see it? In 1998, scientists in England recorded a chick like him. One chick, sounding just like a whole nest of starving babies.

Her eyes saw one. Her ears heard four. So she worked for four.

Mae wasn’t dumb. She was the kindest bird in the reeds. That’s exactly why it worked.

Being kind is your strength. Overdo it, and you lose your own song.

One morning, Mae stopped. Just for a minute. Top of the reeds. And she sang.

So who’s your chick? And when did you last sing?

What strength do you overuse?

The Wait

This mother octopus hasn’t eaten in four years. The food is right there. She won’t leave her eggs.

A shrimp swam up. “Go eat,” he said. “Just one minute.”

“In one minute,” she said, “the crab will eat my babies.”

Down here it’s cold, and in the cold, eggs take years to hatch. Could she last that long?

Year one, she’s strong.

Year two, she gets thin. She still pushes every crab away.

Year three, her skin turns white.

One day, a bright light came down.

A robot arm held out a piece of crab. She didn’t even look.

The shrimp is made up. This octopus is real. Scientists came back 18 times. They never saw her eat.

Then one day, she was gone. Only empty eggshells were left.

Her babies lived. They came out big and strong. She never saw them grow up.

She wasn’t weak. She was the most caring mother in the deep. That’s exactly why she couldn’t stop.

Caring is your strength. Too much, and there’s nothing left.

Maybe you know someone like her. They eat last. They sleep last. They say, “I’m fine.”

The octopus can’t stop. You’re allowed to.

So who do you look after? And when did you last eat first?

When did you last put yourself first?

Chocolate, or Her Friend

Her best friend is locked in this tube. In the other tube, there’s chocolate.

Rats love chocolate. And both doors only open from the outside.

She walks right past the chocolate. She goes to her friend.

She bites the door. It won’t move.

She digs. Still nothing.

She’s tired. She doesn’t stop.

Then she pushes, and the door falls open.

Her friend runs out.

Now they go to the chocolate together.

She opens it. Five pieces.

She could eat them all. A rat on her own usually does.

She looks at the chocolate. She looks at her friend.

She doesn’t eat them all. They share.

These are real rats. In 2011, scientists in Chicago ran this test. Rats freed their trapped friends again and again, and often shared the chocolate. The scientists called it empathy.

So who needs you right now? And what chocolate would you walk past for them?

Who do you show up for?
